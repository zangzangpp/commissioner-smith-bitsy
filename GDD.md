## 1. Overview

### a. Game Concept (2–3 sentences)

Commissioner Smith must secure approval to buy 100 office chairs for $2,000 while following an instruction to save public money. Players carry a proposal between offices, trade budget amendments for signatures, and decide whether to take a personal payment. The political satire comes from making an inflated proposal easier to approve than an economical one, while keeping refusal playable to a complete ending.

### b. Core Concept / Issue in Game Studies

The central issue is how rules and victory conditions shape agency. The institution rewards coalition building, while the opening brief demands economy; players can act on either goal but cannot satisfy both. The game asks whether pursuing an approval screen can make an unreasonable condition feel like a useful step in a plan.

### c. Main Learning/Design Goal

Players should notice their own reasons for accepting the next amendment. The intended response is amused discomfort: the rules make cooperation convenient, but the player still chooses it. Reaching the principled ending must feel like finishing a decision, even though the fictional institution rejects the budget.

## 2. Inspiration and Theoretical Framework

### a. Course Readings / Authors Referenced

Johan Huizinga's “Nature and Significance of Play as a Cultural Phenomenon” describes play's bounded order and the rules that hold within it, then distinguishes the cheat from the spoil-sport who exposes that order's fragility (Homo Ludens, printed pp. 10–12). Smith's refusal uses that distinction as a design question: the player can reject the institution's preferred way of succeeding while remaining inside the game's playable rules. Mia Consalvo's “Cheating” examines pressure to advance and how developers' rewards and permissions can normalize shortcuts that were once disallowed (The Routledge Companion to Video Game Studies, 2023 edition, printed pp. 225, 227). Our game adapts that problem of normalization: inflated amendments and the envelope are permitted program actions, so their fictional corruption must be distinguished from a player cheating the software.

### b. Other References

The visual and comic references are ordinary procurement paperwork, office signage, and the solemn language of committee proceedings. All people, institutions, contracts, and events in the game are fictional. No historical incident or real political figure is represented.

## 3. Game World and Story

### a. Setting / Environment

Eight rooms form a small administrative building: Smith Office (0), Capitol Hall (1), Vellum Contracting (2), Doran District Office (3), Bell Training Office (4), Private Lounge (5), Public Records (6), and Budget Hearing (7). The hall makes the main offices easy to revisit; Records leads to the less public transaction. Desks, chairs, files, plants, doorways, and signs make each room readable without a realistic city map.

### b. Story Summary

Smith receives a routine task: buy 100 office chairs for $2,000 and save public money. The contractor can rename the chairs as strategic equipment, while two senators offer signatures in exchange for a local factory surcharge and an expensive training centre. In a private room, Smith can also accept $500 personally. The player revises the proposal, checks the office ledger, and submits at a hearing. Two signatures bring approval and promotion; a personal payment without enough support makes Smith the scapegoat. Keeping the original proposal and declining personal gain brings rejection with an impeccable paper trail. The chairs remain less important to the institution than the coalition around them.

### c. Main Characters

Commissioner Smith is the player character: a procurement official whose career and initial instruction point in different directions. Ms. Vellum, the contractor, sells terminology as much as furniture and presents the premium package with professional confidence.

Senator Doran seeks factory work for the district and turns a $6,000 surcharge into a defence of employment. Senator Bell seeks a lasting public project and treats a $24,000 training centre as a legacy rather than an accessory to a chair order.

Mr. Soft, the private intermediary, offers Smith $500 and values discretion. Clerk Vale gives directions; Archivist Moss preserves inconvenient records; the hearing clerk explains readiness. Chairperson Grey solemnly equates fiscal responsibility with having enough supporters.

## 4. Gameplay Structure

### a. Rules and Mechanics

Players move, bump characters and documents to read or interact, collect two native items, and walk through exits. The task brief is in Smith's Office; the original quote is in the Contractor's Office. They establish the assignment and the baseline before final submission. Walls and furniture constrain movement, and native exit dialogue checks prerequisites.

Important choices use two interactions: the first previews the cost and consequence; a second interaction with that choice confirms it. Another proposal can replace the pending preview. Conditional dialogue checks existing commitments, so repeated interactions do not duplicate a charge or signature.

The budget starts at $2,000. The optional strategic package adds $8,000 but no vote. The district factory amendment adds $6,000 and Doran's signature; the training centre adds $24,000 and Bell's signature. With both signatures the minimum budget is $32,000, or $40,000 with the premium package. The $500 personal payment does not change the public budget, cannot be returned, and changes the risk at submission; explicit refusal closes that opportunity.

Restoring the original proposal sets the budget back to $2,000, removes both amendments and the premium package, and revokes both signatures. It does not erase a personal payment. Smith can return to the office ledger to inspect budget, commitments, support, personal gain, and the next task.

### b. Objectives and Challenges

The institutional objective is two signatures and approval. The competing objective is an economical purchase without personal gain. No time limit or hidden random roll resolves this conflict; players decide which objective to pursue.

Submission requires the brief and quote, then a preview and confirmation. Ending priority is fixed: B if both signatures are present; otherwise C if Smith accepted the personal payment; otherwise A if the budget is $2,000. A partly amended proposal without the payment cannot be submitted: the clerk asks the player to finish the coalition or restore the original. These conditions prevent a costly unfinished proposal from being labelled principled.

A ends with rejection and an unhelpful evaluation of Smith's coordination skills. B approves the proposal and promotes Smith, even if the envelope was also accepted. C leaves an unsupported Smith responsible while the other participants suddenly defend economy. All three are complete endings; approval is the institution's success condition, not the only legitimate way to finish playing.

### c. Playthrough Summary

Smith begins in the office, collects the brief, and reads the requirement to save money. The ledger and signs explain interaction and the current figures. Following the hall to the contractor supplies the original quote and the first contrast between a plain chair and a costly strategic description.

The player visits Doran and Bell. Each senator has a distinct motive, but both make a signature available through a visible increase in expenditure. Previewing an amendment allows the player to inspect its exact consequences before confirming, then revisit the ledger and see the new budget and support.

Records offers optional paperwork and access to the private room. The player can accept or refuse the personal payment after reading its consequence. Before the hearing, the player can keep building the coalition or restore the original proposal; restoring it also demonstrates that support belonged to the promises rather than to Smith.

At the hearing, the clerk describes whether the current proposal is ready and which outcome its commitments make possible. The player previews and confirms submission, reads the short ending, and can restart with initial state restored. The first-session target is roughly 8–10 minutes with exploration and reading; a familiar player can finish faster, and this target is not a timed user-study result.

## 5. Design and Aesthetic Choices

### a. Visual Style

Office blues, contractor browns and gold, district burgundy, training teal, private purple, records grey, and hearing red and gold distinguish the rooms. The native world uses consistent 8-by-8 pixel drawings within 16-by-16 tile rooms. The suit silhouette, chair backs, folded files, and outlined doors use repeated shapes so players can recognize their function across rooms.

### b. Sound and Text

The game uses silence rather than a soundtrack. Brief English dialogue and formal labels carry the humour: characters describe excess as a sensible administrative necessity. Optional documents and signs reward exploration with smaller jokes, while the ledger and confirmation text use plain factual language so a joke does not hide a cost.

### c. Emotional Tone

The opening feels routine and manageable. Cooperation should become satisfying as signatures accumulate, even as the public budget becomes absurd. The ending interrupts that satisfaction with a short administrative consequence rather than a speech telling the player what to think.

## 6. Technical Plan

### a. Bitsy Implementation

The game uses the authentic Bitsy 8.15 engine, pinned to upstream commit ee412fa8c072d16af4aedd90d4ad3fb7306911b0. The native game.bitsy data and the embedded data in index.html come from the same final build. The HTML contains its engine, font, drawings, text, and game data for offline play; the native data can be reopened in Bitsy for editing. The wrapper adds a restart button; native touch input uses swipes and taps. Presentation and restart controls do not replace the native game rules.

The data contains 8 rooms, 27 sprites including the avatar, 32 dialogue definitions plus opening text, and 2 collectible items. Native items hold the brief and original quote. Repeatable sprite interactions hold proposals and confirmation steps; variables, conditional dialogue, and exit logic manage the state. The state variables are budget, factory, training, premium, perk, refused, submitted, ending, pending, briefed, quotes, and signatures. The flags represent commitments and progress, pending identifies a previewed action, and ending records the final result. The budget is $2,000 + $8,000 * premium + $6,000 * factory + $24,000 * training; signatures is factory + training.

### b. Playability

Twelve automated test groups passed using the unmodified native engine in a Node VM, with only drawing, audio, and input device sinks stubbed. The routes use native input, movement, collision, dialogue, inventory, exits, and the interpreter without directly setting gameplay variables. The checks cover the complete A, B, and C routes, all 16 premium/factory/training/payment combinations and ending priority, exact fees, one-time rewards, refusal, restoration, restart, reachability, and dialogue compilation. Native serialization and reparse passed; the HTML game-data bytes match game.bitsy, and no external runtime assets are required. The test routes and evidence are recorded in TEST_REPORT.md. ## 7. Reflection and Theoretical Connection

### a. Conceptual Rationale

Huizinga's account of an internally binding order helps explain why the approval condition matters. Within this fictional institution, adding unrelated expenditure is a reliable move toward success. The original proposal remains a playable refusal of that institutional order: rejection ends the story rather than trapping the player in an endless demand to cooperate. This is our application of the reading, not a claim that Huizinga described procurement or that the principled player literally breaks the software's rules.

Consalvo's discussion of normalized shortcuts helps frame how an action can move from seeming unacceptable to seeming efficient when a system rewards it. The two signatures give immediate, legible progress, while the public cost rises in a ledger the player can inspect. Because every important cost is disclosed and confirmed, the satire depends on the player's reason for consenting rather than on a surprise penalty. The personal payment adds a separate risk: institutional protection, not merely private wrongdoing, determines whether Smith is promoted or blamed.

### b. Expected Player Experience

Players should leave with a concrete question about their own play: at what point did a questionable amendment become an obvious way to finish the task? The game does not assume everyone will take that route. Comparing a rejected $2,000 proposal with an approved $32,000 proposal makes the conflict visible, and replay lets players test whether they value the approval condition or the original instruction more.
